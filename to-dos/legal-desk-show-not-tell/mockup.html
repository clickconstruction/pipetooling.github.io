Show it, don't say it: four sections of the Legal desk drawn as shapes

The desk's four questions, the account cards and the Timeline's line already show. Four sections still tell: the lien clock's next step and the deadline notes are sentences; the money is a number with a caveat; Evidence is a flat wall of 39 rows; the Timeline repeats each paper's sent to… sentence under its title. Each section below draws today's words beside the proposed shape, then a second-pass box says what the first pass got wrong and what changed.

Second pass · what the first render showed

The first rail was 560 px wide with every label on one line: Jul notice · closed ran under the Last work tick, the retainage and affidavit labels printed on top of each other, and Today sat on the Aug notice label. A rail needs the desk's width and lanes, so pass two gives it the whole row, a gutter that names the three lanes (notices, retainage, affidavit), month rules, and one label per tick placed left or right of it instead of centred. The suit and the serve step come off the rail into two lines under the next press; neither has a decision on it. The gap note is unchanged from pass one.

2 The money bar

A bar cannot double-count: the retainage is drawn inside the bill it sits on, lost profit is the hatched stretch past the open bills, and every offer is a mark on the same scale.

Second pass · what the first render showed

One bar on the claim's scale made the three open bills unreadable slivers — $350 was eleven pixels — because lost profit is three-quarters of the length, and the three offer labels printed over each other. Pass two draws two bars on two scales: the bills alone at full width, where every part reads, and the claim as a thinner bar under it with the bills as one solid block; the offers mark the claim bar on two rows. What's left for us? keeps its own keep / firm / filing bar and still reads on the bills and on the claim, because the firm's cut on a disputed claim is a different decision from its cut on an undisputed balance.

The firm's first three asks — the contract, the notice, the reply — are the first three things on the screen, under headings that say whose paper they are.

The state is a chip, the day is a column and the sentence is one line with ⋯ for the rest; the sent to… record opens on ⋯ and is never repeated on the row.

Grouping needs a whose on each document. Today's rows have a title, a shows line and a state; the group comes from a new one-word field (theirs · ours · the City's · the supplier's · the record), picked when the paper is added, guessed from the title for the rows already there.

Second pass · what the first render showed

The grouping read at once — the reply, the notice and the waiver as the first three rows under Northline's — but every shows line ran off the right edge of the frame and the ⋯ door went with it, because the grid's middle column would not shrink. Pass two lets that column shrink so the line ends in an ellipsis inside the frame and the door stays in view. Grouping by state was tried and dropped in the sketch stage: final is thirty papers from five parties and answers nothing; whose paper it is matches how a lawyer reads a file, and the state still shows as the chip.

The strip is the whole case in a hand's width: the Oct 21 burst, the Oct 27 send, yesterday's reply, two red rings for the overdue steps, three diamonds ahead. The counts stay, as chips with numbers, which is what they are.

A paper on the Timeline is one line and two chips; its sent to… record is a press away, never printed under the title. Papers sent on one day share the day cell.

A strip of days needs a bounded window. Here it is the first entry to the last deadline on the rail; the Dec 2027 suit stays off it, as on the rail.

Second pass · what the first render showed

The strip read as intended — the Oct 21 burst, the send, yesterday's reply, two red rings, three diamonds — and the day labels at one-a-week are enough. What did not read: an overdue row looked like any other row until the eye reached its chip. Pass two gives an overdue row a red rule down its left edge, the same mark the Pipeline uses for a late bill, so the two overdue steps are the first thing the eye lands on under the strip. The strip's day cells cap at four dots so a busy day stays a column, not a smear.
