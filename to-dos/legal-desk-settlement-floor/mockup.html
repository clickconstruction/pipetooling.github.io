The settlement floor: what it does today, and five ways to show what could happen

The owner: not all of the legal items need a settlement floor. Some cases are hard to try and some are easy to try. They should be settled for what they are able to be settled for, and that should be done by the attorney, and we get what we get. If they don't settle it costs us more in collections and in taking them to trial. Then: how could we improve the settlement floor to better represent what is happening and what could happen? Removing it is also an option.

Every name and figure is invented. One matter runs through every frame: the dental clinic on Brazos (job 742), a subcontract under Marlow Commercial, two open bills $14,250.00, retainage no bill asks for yet $2,100.00, so the claim is $16,350.00. Hale & Rowe have it; 33% and $350 to file; the demand is out, no suit yet. Their offer $9,000.00 on Oct 20, our counter $13,500.00 to Nov 5. Today's floor: 80% of the balance, $11,400.00.

What it shows: the floor as a mark beside the offers, so a reader sees at once that their offer sits under it and ours over it.

What it does not: anything about trial. The slider's marks are Any amount · 50% · The full balance. The office can set the floor above what even a full win at trial would net after the firm's cut and its costs, and no screen would say so. The firm's card reads permission, not information.

What it costs: the stage waits on a sign-off, the ask sits on Needs You, and a Not yet leaves no trace of what the refused offer was worth against what came later.

The four questions, the verdict, the write-down panel, the Timeline and both prints never read the floor. Removing it changes one gate, one block, one mark, one card line and one Needs You line.

This is the only direction that draws both halves of the question. What is happening: the offers as they stand. What could happen: each path's net, its time, and the attorney's word on how hard it is.

The walk-away number is derived, not demanded. The claim less the filing cost and the costs to try, divided by what we keep after the firm's cut. It moves as the firm's costs land, and it reads settling beats a full win above this, never the office will not take less.

No invented figure. A chance of winning would be one, so there is none. The read is the attorney's word in two values and a sentence; the rest is arithmetic on numbers the desk holds or the firm types.

The office's say before the deed goes with the gate. What stays: every offer on the bar against the line, Countered on the Offers log, and a question to the firm. If the owner wants a hard say kept for the rare matter, B's Hold a floor door fits inside this block.

One rate. The firm's row carries one contingency percent; firms often charge less before suit than at trial. If Hale & Rowe do, the firm row wants a second rate and the trial path reads it.

A train of four: the gate goes (S) · the read, a migration and one portal act (M) · the paths kernel and the panel (M) · the same panel on the portal and in the printed packet (S).

D Show what the floor has cost: refused offers against how the matter ended

The floor is a bet that holding out nets more. The record can settle the bet: each refused offer against the amount the matter ended at, the costs added since, and the days between. One line per closed matter, and a rollup on the firm's window.

The floor shipped on 2026-10-05. There is almost nothing to read yet, and a closed matter with a refused offer behind it may be a year away.

Not a direction on its own: the lens that proves or disproves the floor over time. Build the one line per matter with whichever direction wins, and the rollup once there are rows. S.

The recommendation: C, with A folded in. The gate goes first, in one small PR, because the owner's words say the attorney settles. Then the read, the paths and the derived line, each a small PR.
