ClickTooling · Bids → Labor · design study · 2026-09-11

Hours that learn

The Labor tab's job, restated: turn a count sheet into hours a plumber believes, then into a budget the job can burn against. Today it is a grid of zeros with an empty rate box. The refresh below keeps the grid and changes what surrounds it: a rate that is never blank, matching that survives real count sheets, a queue instead of a scan, calibration from finished jobs, and a sanity strip that says whether the whole bid makes sense. Frames use B375 SPACEX BA-02N's real rows.

The reframeThe estimate is hours. Dollars are derived. Hours are what a plumber can judge on a count sheet, what the crew controls on site, and what the company can measure afterwards. So the tab optimizes for one thing: getting believable hours onto every row fast, and getting more believable each time a job finishes.
Assumptions this mock-up makes (tell me where I'm wrong)A person costs the bid before pricing, in about five minutes. The labor rate is one company number from People with a per-bid override. Footage rows carry labor per 100 ft. The Robot Default book becomes the book; the human books fold into it as overrides.

The meter is the same object Burn reads. "Usable as a job budget" is the completeness the job's Budget card will show after the win — rows with hours, rate present, materials source. The estimator sees it before the bid goes out; the office sees the same words after.

Rate never blank. The crew rate is People's 90-day average recorded field wage with burden, one company number with a per-bid override. Overhead per field hour comes from the lens you just built and stays a separate tile — the direct budget must not carry overhead twice.

The first calibration is one number, not forty. Before any fixture is recalibrated, the book's total prediction can be checked against every linked finished job: predicted hours versus actual. That multiplier needs no per-fixture attribution, lands the moment six jobs are linked, and is printed on the strip so the estimator reads the honest total today.

The sanity strip judges the whole bid, not a row. Revenue per field hour is the one figure that ties estimating to what actually happened on finished jobs; the history band comes from Job Summary's finished rows. It catches light hours and fat bids alike.

What replaces the amber boxesDriving stays (it is a real direct cost) and reads distance from the bid. Lodging stays, collapsed. Estimator time goes: bid time is recorded on the bid and lives in the overhead pool; the tab shows "12.5 h of bid labor recorded on this bid" as a fact, not a $10 × rows guess.

Nobody scans 26 rows for zeros. The queue holds only what the book could not answer, and each answer is one line: what the code means, the three stage hours, save. Saving writes the alias and the hours into the book, so the next bid with WHA-500 never asks again.

Not everything is a fixture. SAWCUTTING and incidentals are tasks with fixed hours, and a sub's line is a sub. The queue's segmented control names the kind; today the grid forces everything into count × hours per unit and a "fixed" checkbox.

Every filled cell says where it came from. Book by alias, book per 100 ft, your edit with the previous value, and how many finished jobs stand behind the entry. The grid stops being a wall of inputs and becomes a list of claims with evidence.

Matching rule, stated onceExact name → alias → the code's letters before its digits (LAV2 → LAV) → nothing. Aliases count everywhere, including Apply; the old primary-name-only path retires with a note in the architecture doc.

This is the reason the two projects go together. A job linked to its bid gives every fixture in the book a real number: the job's field hours, split across its count rows by the book's own stage weights. Four jobs and the toilet stops being a guess.

Confidence is a count, not a color. "4 jobs agree within 30 %" is something a plumber can argue with; a green dot is not. One job reads amber; none reads "no jobs yet" and leaves the book value alone.

Recalibrate is a proposal, never automatic. The button sets the book with the evidence on screen; the old value stays in the entry's history. Bids already priced keep their hours — the estimate is a snapshot, like the job's budget.

Order of calibrationFirst the bid-level multiplier (predicted vs actual per linked job — robust, no attribution). Then per-fixture, only where the linked jobs differ enough in mix for the split to mean something; the drawer says when they don't.
Where the split rule livesOne kernel, laborActualsByFixture.ts: job field hours × (row's book hours ÷ Σ book hours on that bid) → implied hours per unit per row; excluded below 25 % done or under 8 field hours. Tested against the linked pairs before any UI.

Five identical amber sections become one list with a kind. Equipment, permits, subs, waste, other, and driving are all "a dollar amount by stage with a reason"; the kind is a chip, not a section. Adding one is one row, and the empty state is one line instead of five headers.

Estimator time stops being invented. Bid labor is clocked on the bid and already sits in your overhead pool; the tab reports it as a fact and leaves it out of the direct cost, which is exactly how Burn treats overhead on the job side.

The bottom line is the handoff. "Direct cost of this bid" is the figure the job's Budget card will snapshot on the win, with the breakdown intact — labor hours by stage, materials, subs, other — so the loop from this mock-up to the Burn mock-up is one number with a provenance.

06 · The "is this the best we can do?" pass

What changed between the first cut and this one

v1 — the cleaned-up grid

  • Same grid, alias-aware Apply, the rate box pre-filled from People, direct-cost sections deduplicated into one component.
  • A "Recalibrate" button that averaged finished jobs into the book.
  • Footage rows still zero — "labor rides on the fixtures".
  • Completeness as a percent badge.

Better plumbing, same experience: the estimator still opens a wall of inputs and still has no way to know if 1,412 hours is a good answer.

v2 — what the review added

  • The queue replaces the scan. Only unanswered rows ask; each answer teaches the book (alias + hours) so the same code never asks twice.
  • Kinds for rows that aren't fixtures (task with fixed hours, sub) instead of the "fixed" checkbox.
  • Footage gets labor per 100 ft. 41 % of SPACEX's hours are pipe; zero there is not "riding on fixtures", it is missing.
  • Calibration shows its evidence and its count — jobs, fixtures, range — and proposes, never overwrites; the split rule is one tested kernel.
  • The sanity strip judges the whole bid against finished-job history (revenue per field hour), the one check that catches both light hours and fat bids.
  • Completeness is the Burn budget's completeness, same words before and after the win; estimator time leaves the direct cost.

second pass on v2 (asked again)

  • Per-fixture calibration was circular — it split job hours by the book's own weights. The honest first step is the bid-level multiplier (book predicted vs actual, per linked job), printed on the strip; per-fixture only where the job mix supports it.
  • The sub sheets had vanished from the frame — the crew prints Rough In / Top Out / Trim Set sheets from this tab; they stay, on the totals row.
  • "Learn alias" needed a guard: an alias learned on one bid applies everywhere, so the queue shows the book name it will attach to and the entry keeps a history line ("learned from B375 by Wendi").
