## How to read this paper

This paper is the company's side of what the training courses teach. The courses say what a worker must know. This paper says what the company does, who does it and when work stops. Where a lesson says *the company's rule*, this is where the rule is written down.

Every procedure has the same shape. **Who** does it. **When** it is done. **What** the steps are. **Stop work when** the one line that ends the work. **Where it comes from** names the OSHA rule and the lesson behind it. **Confirm** lists what the owner must check before the paper is handed out.

Every number in this paper came from the OSHA 30 refresher or the Intro to plumbing course. The owner confirms each one against the rule it cites before signing. A number that does not fit the company's work is changed here first and in the lesson second.

The words *the safety lead* mean the person named at the top of this paper. The words *the competent person* mean the person the company names for that job. They can be the same person. Procedure 2 says how.

## Who to call

- **911** for any injury that needs a doctor now, a cave-in, a fire or a person down in a space.
- **811** before any digging. The office makes the call. The crew checks the marks.
- **988** for a crisis, by call or text. Asking someone if they are all right does not plant the idea.
- **[Safety lead's phone]** for any stop-work, any incident and any near miss.
- **[Office line]** when the safety lead does not answer.
- **[Poison control, 1-800-222-1222]** for a chemical in the eyes, on the skin or swallowed.

**Confirm.** The safety lead's phone and the office line.

## 1. The stop-work right

**Who.** Every worker, on every job, at every level.

**When.** The moment a worker sees a danger of death or serious injury that the job's plan did not cover.

**What.**
1. Say *stop* out loud. The work stops.
2. Move everyone away from the danger. Nobody goes back in to get a tool.
3. Call the safety lead. Say what you saw and where.
4. Stay stopped until the safety lead or the competent person has fixed the danger and said so.
5. Nobody is written up, docked or sent home for a stop. A stop that turns out to be nothing was still right.

**Stop work when.** This is the stop-work line itself. A worker who stops the work is doing the job right.

**Where it comes from.** OSHA Act Section 11c and 29 CFR 1977.12 on the right to refuse dangerous work. The refresher's lessons 1 and 3.

**Confirm.** The company's own line on pay for the stopped hours. The draft says the hours are paid.

## 2. Roles

**Who.** The owner, the safety lead, the competent person and every worker.

**When.** The roles are named before the first day on any job. The names stay on the job's record in the app.

**What.**
1. **The owner** owes every worker a workplace free of known hazards, training the worker understands and PPE at no cost. The owner signs this paper.
2. **The safety lead** keeps this paper current, takes every stop-work call, takes every incident report and keeps the records. The safety lead is [Safety lead].
3. **The competent person** is named by the owner for each job that needs one. They know the hazards of that work and have the authority to stop it. Trenching, silica work and ladder inspection each need one.
4. **The crew lead** on a job runs the daily hazard review in procedure 3. They hold the permits in procedures 5 and 7.
5. **Every worker** follows this paper, inspects their own gear, wears the PPE and reports what goes wrong. A worker never fixes a hazard that is above their training. They report it.
6. One person can hold more than one role. On a two-person job the crew lead is often the competent person too. The owner can be the safety lead.
7. A competent person for trenching has been trained in soil types and protective systems. Time on the job alone does not make one.

**Stop work when.** A job that needs a competent person has none on site. The work waits for one.

**Where it comes from.** 29 CFR 1926.32 for the competent person and 1926.20 for the employer's program. The refresher's lessons 1 and 4.

**Confirm.** The safety lead's name. Which crew members are named competent persons for trenching, and where that training record lives.

## 3. The daily job hazard review

**Who.** The crew lead, with every worker on the job.

**When.** Before the first task of the day, and again when anything changes. A new site, new weather, a new chemical or a new task each start it over.

**What.**
1. Stand at the work and walk through the day's steps in order.
2. For each step, name the hazard. Falls, a trench, a tight space, a torch, dust, a live wire, a heavy lift, heat.
3. For each hazard, name the control. The best control removes the hazard. The next changes the work. PPE comes last.
4. Name who holds each control. A control with no owner is not a control.
5. Check the gear the day needs. Cords, ladders, the tester, the four-gas meter, the extinguisher, the harness.
6. Say where the way out is and where the first aid kit is.
7. Write the review in the job's report in the app. A line per hazard is enough.

**Stop work when.** A hazard with no control is found. The step waits until a control is in place.

**Where it comes from.** 29 CFR 1926.20 and 1926.21 for the program and the training. The refresher's lessons 2 and 5.

**Confirm.** Where the review is written. The draft says the job's report in the app. The owner may want a line in the Daily Tasks instead.

## 4. Trenching and excavation

**Who.** The competent person classifies the soil, picks the protective system and inspects the trench. Every worker in a trench checks their own way out.

**When.** Before anyone enters, every day, and again after rain, a vibration or any change in the soil.

**What.**
1. The office calls 811 at least two business days before the dig. The marks are checked on site before the first cut.
2. Within 3 ft of any mark, the crew digs by hand only. Yellow is gas, blue is water, red is electric.
3. The competent person classifies the soil. Clay by a busy road is Type B at best. Wet soil, sand and gravel are Type C.
4. Any trench 5 ft or deeper gets a protective system. A box, shoring or a slope. The competent person picks it from the tables or the maker's data.
5. Under 5 ft, the competent person still looks for cave-in signs. Water, cracks and loads at the edge each call for a system.
6. Any trench deeper than 20 ft is designed by a registered professional engineer. The company does not dig one without the stamp.
7. The box stands at least 18 in above the top of the trench. Spoil and tools stay at least 2 ft back from the edge.
8. Any trench 4 ft or deeper has a ladder, a ramp or stairs within 25 ft of every worker. The ladder is tied and reaches 3 ft above the top.
9. Where bad air is possible, the competent person tests the air before entry and again during the work. A cut gas line, a sewer, an engine nearby or a chemical each make it possible. The four-gas meter reads oxygen, flammable gas, carbon monoxide and hydrogen sulfide. The air permit is good for one day.
10. Water is pumped out before anyone enters. The competent person watches the pump.
11. The competent person inspects the trench daily and after rain. The inspection is written in the job's report.
12. Nobody works under a load or a bucket. Nobody rides the bucket.
13. The rescue plan for a cave-in is 911 first. Nobody drives an injured worker. Nobody digs with their hands.

**Stop work when.** A wall bulges, cracks, boils, heaves, slumps or slides. Water seeps in. The meter alarms. Anyone smells gas. Everyone out and nobody back in until the competent person has fixed the wall and said so.

**Where it comes from.** 29 CFR 1926 Subpart P, 1926.651 and 1926.652. The refresher's lessons 19, 20 and 30.

**Confirm.** The 5 ft, 20 ft, 25 ft and 18 in lines against the current rule. Which crew members are the company's competent persons for trenching. Whether the company owns a four-gas meter and who calibrates it.

## 5. Confined spaces

**Who.** The entry supervisor signs the permit. The attendant stays outside. The entrant does the work. Three people, or two where the supervisor is also the attendant.

**When.** Any time any part of a worker's body goes into a space with limited ways out. A pit, a vault, a tank, a manhole, a sewer or a crawl space. Reaching in for a tool counts.

**What.**
1. The crew lead decides if the space is permit-required. Any one serious hazard makes it one. Bad air, a chance of being buried, a shape that narrows and traps, or anything else that can kill.
2. A crawl space under a house is treated as permit-required until the air is tested. A sewer and a lift station are always permit-required.
3. The supervisor fills in the permit. The space, the work, the date and the hazards. The controls, the names, the air readings, the gear and the rescue plan.
4. The air is tested with a calibrated meter before entry. Oxygen first, then flammable gas, then toxics. Oxygen is good between 19.5 and 23.5 percent. Flammable gas over 10 percent of its lower explosive limit is a no.
5. The space is ventilated before entry and during the work. The meter stays on the whole time.
6. Anything that can flow in or start up is locked out, blanked or capped first.
7. The attendant stays at the opening with no other job. They count who is in, keep in contact and watch the meter.
8. The entrant comes out on any alarm, any symptom or any order from the attendant.
9. The rescue order is fixed. The entrant climbs out. If they cannot, the attendant pulls with the tripod and the winch from outside. If that fails, the attendant calls 911.
10. **Nobody enters a space to rescue a person.** Most people who die in confined spaces died going in after someone else.
11. The permit is cancelled when the work is done and is kept for one year.

**Stop work when.** The meter alarms, the attendant loses contact, or anyone inside feels dizzy, sick or short of breath. Everyone out. The space is not re-entered until the air reads safe and a new permit is signed.

**Where it comes from.** 29 CFR 1926 Subpart AA, 1926.1203 to 1926.1211. The refresher's lessons 13 and 26.

**Confirm.** Whether the company treats every crawl space as permit-required, as drafted. Whether the company owns a tripod and winch, and where the rescue service is. The permit form itself, which the plan in README.md proposes as a paper in the app.

## 6. Ladders and work at height

**Who.** Every worker who climbs. The competent person inspects the ladders.

**When.** Before every climb, and at the yearly inspection.

**What.**
1. Look at the ladder before each use. A cracked rail, a bent rung, a missing foot or a missing label takes it out of service. Tag it and tell the safety lead.
2. Fiberglass only near anything electrical. Aluminum conducts. Wet wood conducts.
3. An extension ladder stands one foot out for every four feet up. It is tied at the top and the bottom and reaches 3 ft above the landing.
4. A step ladder is opened fully with the spreaders locked. Nobody stands on the top cap or the top step. A step ladder is never leaned.
5. Three points of contact on every climb. Tools go up on a belt or a rope.
6. Nobody stands on the top three rungs of an extension ladder.
7. Fall protection is worn at 6 ft and above on any open edge, roof or platform. The anchor holds 5,000 lb per worker.
8. Materials stay 6 ft back from any edge. A hole over 2 in is covered and the cover is marked.
9. A scaffold is inspected by the competent person before each shift. Fall protection on a scaffold starts at 10 ft.
10. A ladder is never a work platform and never a scaffold plank.

**Stop work when.** A ladder fails the look. The work waits for a ladder that passes.

**Where it comes from.** 29 CFR 1926 Subpart X, 1926.1053 for ladders, Subpart M, 1926.501 for fall protection, and 1926.451 for scaffolds. The refresher's lessons 7, 8, 17 and 18.

**Confirm.** The 6 ft and 10 ft lines. Who does the yearly ladder inspection and where it is written.

## 7. Hot work

**Who.** The worker with the torch, and a fire watch who is not the same person.

**When.** Any soldering, brazing, cutting or grinding that makes a flame or sparks.

**What.**
1. The crew lead fills in a hot work permit for the shift and the task. One permit per shift.
2. Move the work to a safe spot if it can be moved. If not, move the flammables. If they cannot move, shield them.
3. Everything that burns is at least 35 ft from the work or under a fire blanket. Insulation, paper, sawdust, a wood floor and a wall cavity each count.
4. A charged extinguisher is within reach before the torch is lit. Its gauge reads in the green.
5. The torch is lit with a striker. Never with a lighter or a match.
6. Cylinders stay upright, secured and capped when not in use. No oil or grease on an oxygen fitting. Fuel gas and oxygen are stored 20 ft apart.
7. The fire watch stays through the work and 30 minutes after the last flame. They watch for smoke, smell and heat, and they can stop the work.
8. In a wall or a ceiling, the fire watch checks the far side and above.
9. No torch in a space that is not ventilated. Fumes from flux and the cleaner can ignite or poison.
10. A press fitting replaces a torch wherever the company allows it near wood, insulation or a tight space.

**Stop work when.** No fire watch, no extinguisher or no permit. Or any smoke or smell from where the flame is not.

**Where it comes from.** 29 CFR 1926 Subpart J, 1926.352 for fire prevention in welding and cutting, and 1926.150 for extinguishers. The refresher's lesson 25.

**Confirm.** The 35 ft line and the 30 minute line. Where the company uses press fittings instead of a torch. The permit form.

## 8. Silica and dust

**Who.** The competent person for silica runs the plan. Every worker who cuts, grinds or drills follows it.

**When.** Any cut, grind or drill in concrete, block, brick, mortar, tile, stone or cast iron pipe. Any time dust is dry swept.

**What.**
1. Water first. A wet saw, a wet core drill or a hose on the cut. Dry cutting concrete is not done.
2. Where water cannot be used, the tool runs with a shroud and a HEPA vacuum.
3. A worker follows OSHA's Table 1 for the task. The table says the control and whether a respirator is needed. Over four hours in a shift, or indoors, the respirator rule is stricter.
4. A respirator is an N95 at least, fit tested once a year, on a worker cleared by a doctor. A dust mask with one strap is not a respirator.
5. Dust is never swept dry and never blown with compressed air. It is wetted and vacuumed.
6. Cast iron pipe is cut with a snap cutter, not a grinder, wherever it can be.
7. In any house built before 1980, a pipe wrap, a floor tile or a ceiling is presumed to hold asbestos. Nobody cuts it. Stop, do not disturb it, and call the safety lead.
8. A written exposure control plan is kept by the safety lead and reviewed once a year.

**Stop work when.** The water stops, the vacuum hose comes off or the shroud clogs. The cut waits until the control is back. Or any suspect asbestos is found.

**Where it comes from.** 29 CFR 1926.1153 for silica and 1926.1101 for asbestos. The refresher's lessons 13 and 16.

**Confirm.** Whether the company has a written exposure control plan and a respirator program. The draft says the safety lead keeps both. Which workers are fit tested and when.

## 9. Hand and power tools, and GFCI on every cord

**Who.** Every worker, before every use.

**When.** Every shift, and before any tool is plugged in.

**What.**
1. Look at every tool and cord before use. A nicked cord, a missing ground prong or a loose guard takes the tool out of service. Tag it and tell the crew lead.
2. Every cord on a job runs through a GFCI. On a wet floor, in a basement, outdoors or at a sink there are no exceptions. A cord with no GFCI is a cord nobody plugs in.
3. The GFCI is tested with its Test button once a month and after any trip.
4. A tool that trips a GFCI every time is tagged out. The GFCI is doing its job.
5. Never carry a tool by its cord. Never unplug by the cord.
6. No homemade cords, no two-prong adapters and no cut-off ground prongs.
7. A guard is never removed. A tool with a faulty guard is out of service.
8. A gasoline tool never runs in a basement, a pit, a crawl space or any closed room. Its exhaust is carbon monoxide.
9. A damaged lithium battery is out of service. Only the maker's charger is used.
10. Eye protection on every cut, every grind and every drill. Hearing protection on any tool that makes you raise your voice.
11. A powder-actuated tool is run only by a worker with the card.

**Stop work when.** A cord with no GFCI is the only cord. The work waits for the GFCI.

**Where it comes from.** 29 CFR 1926.404 for the GFCI, 1926.300 to 1926.307 for tools. The refresher's lessons 9 and 23, and Intro to electrical's lesson 22.

**Confirm.** Whether every truck carries an in-line GFCI. Whether the monthly test is written anywhere.

## 10. Lifting and ergonomics

**Who.** Every worker.

**When.** Before any lift, and every hour during repetitive or vibrating work.

**What.**
1. Plan the lift. Know the weight and the route before the first pull.
2. Over 50 lb, get a second person or a cart, a dolly or a hoist. A water heater is a two-person lift or a dolly, every time.
3. Count off a team lift together. One person says when.
4. Lift from between mid-thigh and mid-chest. Squat, keep the back straight, hold the load close and do not twist.
5. Heavy work sits 4 to 6 in below the elbow. Precision work sits about 4 in above it.
6. Take a break about every hour from repetitive or vibrating work. A jackhammer, a core drill and a long run of overhead drilling each count.
7. Knee pads for floor work. Anti-vibration gloves on vibrating tools.
8. Tell the crew lead at the first ache, numbness or tingling. Early is cheap.

**Stop work when.** A lift cannot be made safely by the people on site. It waits for a second person or a machine.

**Where it comes from.** OSHA's General Duty Clause and the NIOSH lifting guidance the course cites. The refresher's lessons 21 and 27.

**Confirm.** The 50 lb line. The company's own rule for water heaters, which the draft makes a two-person lift or a dolly every time.

## 11. Vehicles and driving

**Who.** Every worker who drives a company truck or their own vehicle on company time.

**When.** Every trip.

**What.**
1. Walk round the truck at the start of the shift. Lights, tires, brakes, wipers, horn and the load. A defect is reported before the truck moves.
2. Press **On my way** in the app before the truck moves. The press clocks you in on the job and texts the customer. There is nothing to type on the road.
3. The phone is not used while driving. Not to call, not to text, not to read. A call waits until the truck is parked.
4. Seat belts on, every seat, every trip.
5. Ladders, pipe and cylinders are tied down. Nothing rides loose in the cab.
6. Back up with a spotter when the view behind is blocked. Sound the horn before backing on a site.
7. On a site, yield to machines. Make eye contact with the operator before walking near one. Stay out of the swing radius.
8. Nobody drives an injured worker to the hospital. That is 911.
9. A worker with a suspended licence, or who has been drinking, does not drive. They tell the crew lead and the crew lead finds a ride.

**Stop work when.** A truck fails the walk-round on brakes, tires or lights. It does not move until fixed.

**Where it comes from.** 29 CFR 1926.601 for motor vehicles and 1926.600 for equipment. The refresher's lesson 24. The On my way press is described in the guide *job mode and clocking*.

**Confirm.** The company's own line on hands-free calls. The draft says no phone use at all while driving.

## 12. Heat and cold

**Who.** The crew lead watches the crew. Every worker watches their partner.

**When.** Any day over 80°F and any day in a hot attic or a crawl space. Any day under 40°F or wet.

**What.**
1. Water is on the truck every day. Drink all day, not only when thirsty.
2. In the heat, a break in the shade at least every two hours. More in an attic.
3. A new worker, or anyone back from a week away, takes one to two weeks to get used to heat. The crew lead gives them lighter work for that time.
4. Know the stages. A rash, then cramps, then exhaustion with heavy sweat and a headache, then heat stroke. Confusion and hot dry skin mean heat stroke.
5. Heat stroke is 911. Move the person to shade and cool them with water while you wait.
6. In the cold, dry clothing matters more than warm clothing. Wet feet lose heat 25 times faster. A change of socks rides on the truck.
7. Shivering, confusion and slurred speech are hypothermia. Warm area, dry clothes, warm sweet drinks and medical care. Never alcohol.
8. On a wet day, a cord on a wet floor is procedure 9.

**Stop work when.** Anyone shows confusion. The work stops and the call is made.

**Where it comes from.** OSHA's General Duty Clause and the heat guidance the course cites. The refresher's lesson 15.

**Confirm.** The 80°F line and the two-hour line. The company's own attic rule in summer.

## 13. Hazard communication

**Who.** The safety lead keeps the sheets and the labels. Every worker reads them before a new chemical is used.

**When.** Before any chemical is used the first time, and any time a container is filled from another.

**What.**
1. Every truck carries a safety data sheet for every chemical on it. A binder, or the app's company documents page. The sheet is there on every job.
2. The sheets cover at least these. PVC and CPVC primer and cement. ABS cement. Solder and flux. Pipe thread sealant. Drain chemicals and enzyme cleaners. Torch fuel. Degreaser. Penetrating oil. Caulk and foam.
3. Every container keeps its label. A chemical poured into another container gets a label with the name and the hazard before it leaves the truck.
4. Primer, cement and drain chemicals are used with the window open or a fan on. Their vapors are the hazard. Gloves and glasses on.
5. Primer and cement are kept closed and away from the torch. They burn.
6. Drain chemicals are never mixed. Acid and lye together make heat and gas. A drain that already has a chemical in it is told to the crew before anyone opens it.
7. Sewage is treated as infectious. Gloves, glasses and a change of clothes. Cover every cut. Wash before eating.
8. Rodent droppings, mold and a wasp nest are each reported before the work goes on. The nest is removed by someone else.
9. Nobody disturbs anything that may be asbestos or lead paint. Procedure 8 holds.
10. Every worker is shown where the sheets are and how to read a label when they start.

**Stop work when.** A chemical with no sheet and no label is on the job. It goes back on the truck until it has both.

**Where it comes from.** 29 CFR 1910.1200, the hazard communication standard, and 1926.59. The refresher's lesson 14.

**Confirm.** The list of chemicals the trucks carry. Where the sheets live, which the plan in README.md proposes as the app's company documents page.

## 14. PPE by task

**Who.** The company buys it. The worker inspects it every day and wears it.

**When.** Always, as the table says.

| Task | Wear |
|---|---|
| Every job | Boots with a safety toe. Safety glasses. Gloves for the task. |
| Any site with overhead work or machines | A hard hat. Class G or better where wiring may be hidden. |
| Cutting, grinding or drilling | Safety glasses. A face shield over them on a grinder. Hearing protection. |
| Silica dust | The control in procedure 8 first. An N95 or better where Table 1 says so. |
| Torch work | Safety glasses with a shade. Leather gloves. Nothing synthetic on the skin. |
| Primer, cement or drain chemicals | Chemical gloves rated for them. Goggles, not glasses, when it can splash. |
| Sewage or a flooded space | Chemical gloves. Goggles. Boots that seal. A change of clothes. |
| A trench or a confined space | A harness where the rescue plan says so. The meter. |
| Work at 6 ft or more on an open edge | A harness, a lanyard and an anchor rated 5,000 lb. |
| Crawl spaces | Knee pads. A headlamp. Coveralls. |
| Wet floors and any sink work with a cord | The GFCI. Rubber soled boots. |

1. A hard hat is replaced after any hit, after two years of heavy use or five years from its date. The suspension is replaced every year.
2. Safety glasses are not prescription glasses. Prescription safety glasses are ordered through the office.
3. Damaged PPE goes to the safety lead for a replacement the same day.
4. A worker does not pay for PPE. A worker who loses it tells the safety lead and gets another.

**Stop work when.** The PPE the table names is not on the truck. The task waits for it.

**Where it comes from.** 29 CFR 1926 Subpart E, 1926.95 to 1926.106. The refresher's lessons 12 and 13.

**Confirm.** The whole table. It is a draft of what a plumbing crew wears and the owner knows the trucks.

## 15. Incident and near-miss reporting

**Who.** Every worker reports. The crew lead calls. The safety lead records.

**When.** At once. Before the end of the shift at the latest.

**What.**
1. An injury that needs more than a bandage is 911 first. Then the safety lead.
2. Every injury, every unsafe condition and every near miss is reported to the crew lead the same hour. A near miss is when someone almost got hurt. It is reported like an injury.
3. The crew lead calls the safety lead. The report is written in the job's report in the app before the shift ends.
4. The safety lead keeps the OSHA 300 log if the company has more than 10 employees. The 300A summary is posted from February 1 to April 30 and kept for five years.
5. A death is reported to OSHA within 8 hours. A hospital stay, an amputation or a lost eye within 24 hours. The safety lead makes the call.
6. The scene of a serious incident is left as it is. It stays that way until the safety lead has seen it or said otherwise.
7. The safety lead looks for the cause, not the blame. A report never costs the worker who made it.
8. Any shock, however small, gets a doctor's look the same day.
9. A worker may call OSHA at any time. Nobody is punished for it. A retaliation complaint goes to OSHA within 30 days.

**Stop work when.** This procedure has no stop line of its own. The procedure that was broken has it.

**Where it comes from.** 29 CFR 1904 for the log and the reports, and 1904.39 for the 8 and 24 hour calls. The refresher's lessons 1, 3 and 29.

**Confirm.** Whether the company is over 10 employees and keeps the log. Where the incident report is written, which the plan in README.md proposes as the job's report.

## 16. Training required

**Who.** The owner sets the policy. The safety lead watches the standings. Every worker takes the courses.

**When.** Before the first day on a crew, and again when the course comes due.

**What.**
1. The courses are the ones the policy in Settings → Training names. Today they are the OSHA 30 refresher, Intro to plumbing and Intro to electrical.
2. Each course says on its card which roles must take it. A course the card lists as Available is open to everyone and required of nobody.
3. A pass is good for the months the policy sets. The card turns amber thirty days before a pass runs out and red when it has.
4. A worker who is red on a required course works beside one who is green. That holds until the pass is back. The crew lead sets it up.
5. The safety lead reads the standings on the People page once a month.
6. A new rule in this paper goes into the lesson it belongs to. A new number in a lesson goes into this paper. The two are kept the same.
7. The OSHA 30 refresher is not an OSHA card. The card comes from the 30-hour course. Outside cards stay on People → Licenses.
8. Trenching competent persons, confined space attendants and respirator wearers each have training beyond the courses. The safety lead keeps those records.

**Stop work when.** A job that needs a trained role has nobody on site with that training.

**Where it comes from.** 29 CFR 1926.21 for training, and the policy in Settings → Training. The guide *take a training course*.

**Confirm.** Which roles must take which course, which is the policy in Settings → Training. The draft's line that a red worker works beside a green one.

## 17. Keeping this paper current

1. The safety lead owns this paper. A change is made here first.
2. The paper is read again once a year, and after any serious incident.
3. A change that affects a lesson goes into the course in the same week.
4. The crew is told of a change at the next daily review. The date at the top moves.
5. The signed copy lives where the plan in README.md puts it. Every worker can open it from the app.
