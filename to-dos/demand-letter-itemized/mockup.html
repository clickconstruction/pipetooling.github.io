What the law asks of the letter

Texas has no statutory form for a contractor's demand letter, but five rules decide what a good one contains and what a bad one costs. Cites are to the Texas statutes and rules; confirm the two marked attorney with counsel before the wording ships.

RuleWhat it asks of the letterCite
Sworn accountA suit on an account needs a systematic, itemized record: the name, date and charge of each item, with all payments and credits allowed. The letter's debt section should be that record — the invoice lines, not a summary — and the invoice itself should ride along as the exhibit.Tex. R. Civ. P. 185; Panditi v. Apostle, 180 S.W.3d 924 (Tex. App.—Dallas 2006)
Attorney's feesFees on a claim for services, labor, materials, a sworn account or a contract require the claim to be presented and unpaid for 30 days. The demand letter is the presentment; the 30-day clock runs from it, whatever pay-by date the letter names.Tex. Civ. Prac. & Rem. Code § 38.001, § 38.002
Debt collectionTexas's act binds the original creditor, not only agencies — but only for a consumer debt, so a homeowner's letter is regulated and a GC's is not. It forbids threatening an action not intended or not lawfully available, misrepresenting the amount or character of the debt, and adding charges no agreement or statute authorizes. So: the amount must equal the bill; a lien may be named only while the window is open and the property can carry one; a criminal reference is a hazard, not a lever.Tex. Fin. Code § 392.001, § 392.301(a)(2),(6),(8), § 392.303(a)(2), § 392.304(a)(8),(12),(13) attorney
Excessive demandDemanding more than is owed, or signalling that a tender of the true amount would be refused, forfeits the fee claim. The letter must demand the bill's exact balance — one more reason the debt section reads the bill instead of being typed.Findlay v. Cave, 611 S.W.2d 57 (Tex. 1981)
Interest basisThe Prompt Payment chapter covers every contract to improve real property, homeowner jobs included — there is no residential carve-out (§ 28.003 is only the good-faith-dispute withholding rule). An owner or GC who receives a written payment request owes it by the 35th day; from the day after, the unpaid amount bears 1.5 % a month, and the court may award fees. Where no written payment request was made, the fallback is the legal rate: 6 % a year from the 30th day after due. "Late fees may accrue" names neither.Tex. Prop. Code § 28.001, § 28.002(a), § 28.004, § 28.005; Tex. Fin. Code § 302.002
Who is demanded ofA demand for payment goes to the party in contract. On a job with a GC, the bill went to the GC and so does the demand; the owner gets the § 53.056 notice, which lets the owner withhold from the GC and makes the owner liable for money paid out afterward. The old § 53.083 demand to the owner was repealed for contracts from 2022 on, so the notice is the only owner-facing instrument — and the statute says it may enclose the invoice.Tex. Prop. Code § 53.056(a-2),(a-3), § 53.081, § 53.084; § 53.083 repealed by HB 2237 (2021)
Theft of serviceThe presumption of intent needs a written demand by certified or registered mail with return receipt (or a commercial delivery service), to the address on the service agreement, unpaid 10 days after receipt — the source of the app's 10-day default. Stays off until the attorney package: to a homeowner, a letter that threatens a criminal charge over a good-faith dispute is exactly what § 392.301(a)(2) and (a)(6) forbid.Tex. Penal Code § 31.04(a)(4), (b)(2), (c) attorney
Small claimsJustice court hears claims to $20,000. The line is true on this job and most jobs; the letter should say the court, not "small claims".Tex. Gov't Code § 27.031

Is this the best we can do?

  1. It still demands from the wrong person. The bill on this job went to RMC- Dudley Mason under the who-pays rule; the letter's recipient logic ignores that rule and falls back to the job customer. Draft A itemizes beautifully for a homeowner who was never billed. The party has to come from the invoice, the same way the invoice picked it.
  2. The table is retyped, not read. A description that can be edited on the letter is a description that can drift from the bill. The only reason to edit the debt is that the bill is wrong — and then the bill is what gets fixed, so the sworn-account record stays one record.
  3. "Attach the invoice" is a checkbox, so some letters won't have it. The invoice is the evidence. It is not optional, and the preview should show it as the page it will be, stamped as the exhibit, not as a filename in a footnote.
  4. Two clocks, one of them silent. The letter names September 28 (10 business days, borrowed from § 31.04 which is switched off). The fee clock under ch. 38 is 30 days from this letter and the letter never says so — the one date that changes what a lawyer can recover is missing.
  5. "Late fees may accrue" is the line the Debt Collection Act dislikes. It asserts a charge without a basis. The basis exists: § 28.004 for a GC contract, the § 302.002 legal rate for a homeowner with no written rate, or the contract's own rate when one is on file. The letter should name one.
  6. The lien line is a promise the app can already check. The Lien window knows the filing window and whether the property is a homestead; the letter offers the line regardless. A threatened lien that cannot be filed is exactly § 392.301(a)(8).
  7. Notice History cites the app's own log, not what the debtor received. "Invoice delivered to customer" is a Stripe event. Worth keeping, but the exhibit is what proves it; the history should point at the exhibit pages.

Refined B — the letter reads the bill, and carries it

Three changes of principle: the debtor and the debt come from the invoice and are not editable here; the invoice is always Exhibit A and the preview shows it; every legal line names its basis or is not offered.

The record it leaves

One job_demand_letters row, as today, plus: which invoices it claims (invoice_ids already exists and is now always set), the party demanded of (debtor_party customer · gc · owner, with the customer id), the exhibits and their page counts (exhibits jsonb), the fee-clock date (fee_clock_date), and the interest basis the letter named. The Legal desk's Paper tab and the firm's portal read those fields, so a matter arrives with the presentment date and the exhibits already in evidence.

Is this the best we can do?

  1. Two dates on one letter can still confuse a reader. B keeps the 10-business-day pay-by and adds the 30-day fee clock in plain words. The alternative is a single 30-day deadline, which is simpler to read and safer for fees, but a month is a long time to wait on $1,710. This is the owner's call; B ships with both dates and the default pay-by unchanged. decision
  2. The homeowner is not forgotten — but B moves them to a different tool. On a GC job the owner never gets the demand; they get the notice, and it is due tomorrow on this very job. B puts that one sentence and door in the recipient block, because the person opening this modal is thinking "make them pay", and the law's answer for the owner is the notice. The notice may enclose the invoice (§ 53.056(a-3)), so the same Exhibit A machinery belongs on the notice tab and in the Lien desk run — a fifth, small PR. There is no demand to the owner any more: § 53.083 was repealed for contracts from 2022 on.
  3. Read-only debt is a strong choice. It removes a real use: a partial demand, or a demand that adds a charge the bill did not. The answer is that both belong on the bill first (a new line, a credit), never on the letter alone. B keeps "Fix the bill ›" one click away and the letter re-reads after the fix.
  4. Exhibit A is the app's invoice PDF, not the Stripe page. The customer may have seen the Stripe-hosted bill. The app's physical-invoice renderer produces the same number, lines and dates from the same rows, and it is what the app can print. B labels it "as sent Aug 18" and keeps the pay link on it. If the office wants the Stripe rendering instead, that is a later fetch of Stripe's own PDF, not a blocker.
  5. Email is new and it changes the record. The notice already emails from the app; the demand letter is record-only, by the earlier "keep the legal path physical" reading. B adds Email as a second channel, never the default, because certified mail is what § 31.04 and the return receipt want. It reuses the notice's edge function pattern; a send by email is recorded as one. decision
  6. What B does not do. It does not write the letter in Spanish, does not know the GC's registered agent, and does not read the bill's "payment due on receipt" as a contract term. Those are the attorney package and the customer-terms work, already listed.

The plan — four PRs, smallest first

PR 1 · the letter reads the bill
  • Kernel: statement-of-account blocks from the invoice document model (number, sent/due dates, lines, credits, balance), one block per invoice, a total when several.
  • Recipient from effectiveInvoiceParty — the same rule the bill used; the "who owes it" block with the notice pointer on GC jobs.
  • The invoice number is the display number, never an id fragment.
  • Tests for the blocks (one invoice, two, credits, a discount line); the old free-text fields retired.

Decisions for the owner

  1. The deadline: keep 10 business days as the pay-by and state the 30-day fee clock beside it (proposed), or make the letter's one deadline 30 days?
  2. Email from the app: add it as a second channel, certified mail still the default (proposed), or keep the demand letter record-only?
  3. The homeowner on a GC job: the demand goes to the GC and the owner gets the notice (proposed, per the statute), or keep a switch to address the owner directly?
  4. For the attorney: whether the bill counts as the chapter 28 "written payment request" on a homeowner job (so 1.5 % a month applies there too), whether the ch. 38 sentence belongs on every letter, and the § 31.04 line.
