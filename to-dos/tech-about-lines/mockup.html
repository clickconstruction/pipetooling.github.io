The tech's introduction: a short resume that rides the first On my way a tech sends a customer

The office writes a short introduction for Luis, the way a resume reads when you hand it over: years in the trade, the license, what he is best at, a personal line. The first time Luis presses On my way to Dana, his text goes as today and his introduction follows it as a second text. Every later On my way from Luis to Dana is just the line. When Michael takes the next visit, Dana gets Michael's. The people, the job and the introductions are made up.

4 The calls

Five, Grace's. The recommendation is first in each.

  1. One text or two. The introduction as its own text right after the On my way line recommended, or one longer text with the introduction under a blank line. Two reads like a card handed over after the greeting, keeps the On my way line exactly the office's words, and lets the line go even when the introduction cannot.
  2. What counts as introduced. A line in the customer's thread that introduces this tech recommended, so a tech whose first text was Running late still introduces themself on their first On my way; or any earlier text from this tech to this customer. And whether a tech is introduced again after a long gap: never recommended, or after a year with no texts to that customer.
  3. The voice. The company's words about the tech, third person, opened by A little about Luis: recommended, or the tech's first person, A little about me: I have…. Third person matches the handover; the office writes the pronoun in, so there is nothing to guess.
  4. May a tech write? Turn a line off only recommended, or suggest one the office approves, or write freely. The veto keeps the company's voice and ships smallest; a suggestion door is one later cut.
  5. Where the office edits. The person's desk recommended, or a card on Settings → Phone and texts listing every tech. The desk is where the office already shapes who a person is, one person at a time.

5 Alternatives weighed

  1. One fact rotated into every On my way (the first mock-up).rejected Grace: the introduction is a handover, not a running feature; a customer who knows the tech needs no fact.
  2. The introduction inside the On my way line, through a new blank.rejected It ties the resume to one line's wording, needs an optional blank in the fill system, and lengthens the line. A second text leaves both alone.
  3. One bio field, free text, appended whole.rejected No Spanish beside each line, no veto on a line, no count the office can see.
  4. The tech writes their own introduction in Settings.rejected for now The voice customers hear is the company's. The tech reads every line and can turn one off.
  5. Fields the app composes (years, license, specialty) into sentences.rejected Stiff grammar and the pronoun problem; the office writes a better sentence than a template fills.
  6. The office's lines about each tech, joined into one introduction, sent as a second text the first time a tech texts On my way to a customer, remembered in the thread, vetoed in Settings.picked
  7. A link to a "Meet Luis" page with a photo (Housecall Pro's way).not now The customer's page link is off the phone and there is no photo column. The first follow-on once the On my way customer-page card exists: the introduction then ends with the link.

6 Is this the best we can do? The second pass

The first pass after Grace's correction kept the resume inside the On my way text through a blank and asked "has this tech texted this customer?" Read back against the customer who gets the text, the tech it introduces, and the office that writes it, six things changed.

  1. Inside the line became a second text. The On my way line goes exactly as the office wrote it every time; the introduction follows as its own bubble the first time, like a card after the greeting. The thread shows Luis's introduction as its own line.
  2. "Has texted before" became "has been introduced". A tech whose first text was Running late still gets introduced on their first On my way; an office text does not count as the tech; one column on the message is the memory, read the same way on the phone and the server.
  3. First person became the company's third person. A resume is handed over by someone else. A little about Luis: he has… is the handover's frame, and the office writes the pronoun in.
  4. Prompts for facts became prompts shaped like a resume. Years in the trade, license, best at, then the personal lines: what makes a customer comfortable before the truck pulls up.
  5. A cap per line became a cap on the whole. One text, 320 characters, two segments; the desk counts down on the whole, and the tech's switches change the count.
  6. Nothing on the sheet became a second bubble and one link. The tech reads what Dana gets before it goes, and Not this time holds the introduction for a customer who clearly knows them, while the line still goes.
