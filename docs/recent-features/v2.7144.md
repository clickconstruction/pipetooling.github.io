# v2.7144 — Messages: the mail run names every sender before it files (2026-11-13)

Punch list #332, un-parked on the owner's word the day it was parked ("let's also do #332"): the customer's contact step, the office-hours refusal, a rehearsal of every command against a made-up seat, and a recount. **Nothing was filed.** No migration, no function to deploy, no screen changes.

## Why

v2.7084 left one thing to build before the pilot. A tag lands each mail's line on the contact that holds its sender's address, and makes an unnamed sender, a *Who is this?* row, when no contact does. The first count found that 59 of the 67 outside addresses on the sure conversations had no contact. Filing them would have put dozens of unnamed senders into Messages for people the office knows by name.

## What was built

- **The contact step** (`src/lib/myMail/backfillContact.ts`, pure). For each mail a tag would write, it reads whose thread the line lands on, by the tag's own rule (`tagPlan.ts`'s `partyOf`): a mail in lands on its sender, a mail of ours on the first person outside it was written to, a mail between teammates on its sender's own thread. A conversation is **named** when every such address has a contact that is not a *Who is this?* row. Otherwise it is **held**, with one of four reasons and the press that fixes it: the customer has no contact yet, the address is on the customer's record and on no contact, an unnamed sender already holds it, or nobody the app knows holds it.
- **What the run cannot see is read every way.** The app's own sender and a box kept in a secret are in no table, and an alias is ours to a person and outside to the tag. An address at our domain that is neither a teammate nor a box Settings names is unclear, and every address a line could land on under any reading has to be named. The step holds too much sooner than too little.
- **`plan` names who each mail is filed under.** It asks the seat for its dry run, reads every mail that dry run would write (`read_mail`, read-only), and runs the step. A named conversation gets its `plan` line. A held one gets a `held` line, which `apply` never reads. The sheet is written for someone who knows the job: the job with its address, the conversation's dates, the mailbox, why it is sure in words, who it is filed under, who is not named and the press, the mails, the lines and the files by name.
- **`count` runs the step too**, over the mails its searches found, and writes a second sheet with a job a row for picking the pilot.
- **`apply` refuses in office hours**: 6 am to 7 pm Central, Monday to Saturday (`inOfficeHours`). The runbook asked for this and the code did not hold it. There is no flag past it.
- **`apply` stops at an unnamed sender.** The seat's reply says when a filing made one. The run marks the ledger line and goes no further.

## How a sender gets named, and what was not built

The run makes no contact. The card had left open how the runner would call `contact_for_job` and `contact_for_customer`. Read on main:

- Both write, and the seat's `call_read` goes over GET, which the database runs read-only. The runner cannot call either without a new seat verb.
- `contact_for_job` is a tech's door (`messages_tech_on_job`). It is not the office's or a dev's.
- `contact_for_customer` makes one contact a customer and copies one address onto it, `contact_info.email`. It adds nothing for a billing address or a person at the customer, and adds nothing at all once the contact exists.
- The app has no press that adds an email address to a contact. An address is named after a mail from it lands, in *Who is this?*.

So the press is a person's: the speech bubble beside a customer's name opens their thread, which makes their contact, and the next `plan` reads it. **A seat verb that makes a customer's contact was not built.** It would be a dev-seat change, and the recount says how many presses it would save.

## The recount (2026-11-13, read-only)

160 searches over 13 mailboxes, 119 open jobs, 5,445 conversations. It barely moved in a day, which is what a floor of old mail should do.

| | First count | Recount |
|---|---|---|
| Sure | 93: street 65, subject 20, one-job customer 8 | 94: street 66, subject 20, one-job customer 8 |
| Held for a person | 2,659 | 2,666: several jobs 757, a shared street 614, two jobs named 493, also bids 450, street with a stranger 318, company only 34 |
| Left out | 2,699 | 2,685 |

The 94 sure conversations hold 306 mails on 20 jobs, at most 15 on one. The contact step over them, by the mails the searches found:

| | Conversations |
|---|---|
| Every sender named, ready to plan | **17**, on 5 jobs. On 2 jobs every sure conversation is ready. |
| Held: the customer has no contact yet | 17. One press each on 5 customers would name them all. |
| Held: on the record, on no contact | 8 |
| Held: on *Who is this?* already | 1 |
| Held: nobody the app knows | 51 |

Read from the sheet, the 51 are 47 with a sender outside the company and 4 with only an address of ours. The run now tells those apart (`oursNoContact`). The unnamed senders are 34 addresses in all. 25 of them nobody knows: 20 at a company's own domain, 2 at an open mail service, 3 at ours. The street rule calls anyone at the customer's company a party to the job, and most of these are such people, written to by name and kept on no record.

The first count's 59 unnamed senders counted every outside address on a mail. A line lands on one of them, so the number that matters is 34.

So the rule is sure of 94, and the app can name the senders of 17 today and 34 after five presses. The other 60 wait on people the app has never been told about.
