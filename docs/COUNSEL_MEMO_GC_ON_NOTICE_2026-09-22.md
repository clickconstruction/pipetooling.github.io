Re:  Owner cover letters, payment paths when the GC is silent or already paid out, and the deadlines that still collect
How far persuasion can go on this envelope
This page rides with a statutory notice. It can explain, warn, and make payment easy. It cannot pretend the owner hired us, cannot order the owner to send Click a check that belongs to the GC, and cannot accuse the GC of a general practice of stiffing subs. After HB 2237 repealed § 53.083 there is no statute that forces the owner to pay the sub directly if the GC stays silent.
What does compel an owner is narrower and stronger: after this notice, further payments to the GC can attach to the property if a lien is later perfected and reduced to judgment (§ 53.084). Withholding under § 53.081 is how they stop that. Getting Click a check or wire — from the GC, or from the owner after the GC authorizes it in writing — is how they end it. Click cannot deposit a joint check, so that path is off the page.
Do not put on this page:
“Pay us directly and deduct it from the GC.”

“We have reason to believe the GC is not paying subcontractors generally.”

Attorney’s-fee or theft-of-service language. Those belong on a demand to the GC, not on an owner § 53.056 packet.

A claim amount that silently folds in months whose notice deadline has already died.

Persuasion rules used in the drafts
Owner-protective frame. You are handing them a tool, not serving them with a grievance.

One number. {{amount}} is only the timely, trapable claim. Stale months go in a one-line footnote so the owner sees the real job balance without poisoning the form.

One deadline that is real. “Before you make the next payment to <GC>.” Not a fake 10-day ultimatum.

Three doors, none of them a joint check. GC pays us this week / owner withholds and calls us / owner pays us only with the GC’s written authorization.

Immediate release. Owners fear a lien they cannot get off the record. Promise the paper the day funds clear.

A person, not a company. The master plumber signs and is the callback. That is more persuasive than “accounts receivable.”

When the GC will not answer
That is the current problem, and the 2022 amendments made it worse for the sub. Old § 53.083 said: demand the owner, copy the GC, and if the GC did not dispute within 30 days the owner shall pay the sub. HB 2237 repealed that. GC silence no longer opens a statutory pipe from the owner’s account to Click.
What still works when the GC is a brick wall:
Withholding does not need the GC. § 53.081 lets the owner trap the claim the day the notice arrives. § 53.082 makes them hold it until the lien-affidavit window closes or, if you file, until the claim is paid or released. The owner can do that whether the GC answers or not.

The lien calendar is the leverage. Derivative claimant affidavit: 15th day of the fourth month after last labor or materials on commercial work, 15th day of the third month on residential (§ 53.052). Once that affidavit is of record, the owner is looking at a cloud on title and, if they kept paying the GC after this notice, personal exposure under § 53.084.

Copy the GC anyway. Every owner letter, every payment ask, every release goes to the last known GC address by the same traceable mail. Silence is not consent, but a record that you did not go around them is what you want if they later wake up and cry interference.

Direct owner payment without a GC email is the owner’s call, not yours to order. When the GC has gone dark, many owners will pay the trapped amount to the sub and take a release to clear the property. You can make that easy. You should not tell them the Code requires it.

If the owner already paid the GC in full before this notice, say so immediately. There is nothing left to trap except retainage they should still be holding under Subchapter E. The remaining path is the affidavit — not a speech about the next draw.

So the GC-silent letter below does three things: tells the owner to stop the next payment to the GC, gives them a way to finish it if they choose to pay Click against a release, and dates the next step — the affidavit — so they know this does not sit forever.

If the owners have already paid the GC in full
Treat that as a working theory, not a fact. Click does not see the owner–GC draw sheet. Some owners have paid out. Some still owe a final draw or the statutory 10%. Some released retainage early. The first job this week is to find out which is which, job by job, and to stop the clocks that still run.
What money is still reachable
There are three pots. They are not the same.
Future payments the owner still owes the GC. § 53.081 traps those only after the owner has the § 53.056 notice. If the owner already paid the GC every dollar before that notice arrived, this pot is empty.

Statutory reserved funds (the 10%). § 53.101 makes the owner reserve 10% of the original-contract price during construction and for 30 days after that contract is completed. That duty exists whether or not Click has sent a notice. If the owner paid the GC 100% and never reserved the 10%, § 53.105 gives complying claimants a lien at least to the extent of what should have been reserved. Several unpaid subs share that 10% pot.

The property itself. A timely notice plus a timely affidavit is a lien on the lot and the improvements. That is the asset when the GC is gone and the owner has paid the GC in full. Owners who “already paid” still refinance, sell, and sit through closings. A recorded affidavit is what gets their attention.

§ 53.084(a) is the sentence that hurts here: except for reserved funds the owner failed to hold, the owner is not liable for amounts paid to the GC before the owner was authorized to withhold. Do not write these owners as if they still owe Click the full invoice as a contract debt.

How payment actually comes in on a paid-out book
From the GC, if anything is left — separate demand, Chapter 28, suit on the subcontract. Do not count on it if they have gone dark across multiple jobs.

From an owner who still holds a last draw or the 10% — that is the cleanest owner money. The letter above is how you find it.

From an owner who holds nothing but wants the affidavit off the record before a sale or refinance — they pay to take the cloud off. That is why you file promptly on Pile C.

From a title company at closing — same cloud. Send the payoff and a release-to-follow.

From a payment bond, if one exists on that project — calendar the bond claim the same day you calendar the affidavit.

What does not collect, once the owner has paid the GC in full before this notice: a cover letter that talks as if they still owe you the whole invoice as their own bill. The 10% question and the affidavit date are what make them call.

Trapping retainage across multiple properties
Click cannot throw one net over the whole book. Reserved funds and retainage live on a single original contract, which means a single property. Each job is its own notice, its own 10% pot, and its own affidavit.
Two pots, two notices
Reserved funds (Subchapter E). The owner must reserve 10% of the owner–GC contract during the work and for 30 days after that original contract is completed (§ 53.101). That fund exists whether Click writes or not. It secures unpaid trades on that project, not Click alone. If the owner failed to reserve it, complying claimants get a lien to the extent of what should have been reserved (§ 53.105). Multiple claimants share it (§ 53.104).

Click’s contractual retainage. The dollars the GC held back from Click under the subcontract. That is “retainage” as the Code now uses the word. It is only the amount the subcontract says is retained (§ 53.025).

§ 53.057 is required for a retainage lien only to the extent unpaid retainage was not already included in a § 53.056 notice. Belt and suspenders: put unpaid retainage in the § 53.056 claim amount while the job is open, and send the § 53.057 form within 30 days after Click’s contract on that job is completed, terminated, or abandoned — or within 30 days after the original contract is terminated or abandoned, whichever comes first.
When the owner may actually withhold
After a § 53.056 notice: immediately on receipt (§ 53.081(b)), and in addition to reserved funds.

After a § 53.057 retainage notice alone: not immediately. The owner may withhold on receipt of a copy of Click’s filed lien affidavit (§ 53.081(c)). That is why a retainage-only notice without a prompt affidavit is a weak trap.

If the goal is to freeze money now, the retainage dollars need to be inside the § 53.056 notice, not waiting on § 53.057.

Playbook for this GC, property by property
Build a grid: job number, address, owner of record, county, commercial vs residential vs homestead, Click contract price, retainage percent and dollars still held, last month Click worked, whether Click’s work is finished, whether the owner–GC contract is still open, whether the owner still owes the GC anything, 53.056 deadline for each unpaid month, 53.057 deadline, 53.052 affidavit date.

One envelope per property. Never staple two owners, two addresses, or two original contracts under one notice.

On each open job, send § 53.056 now for every timely unpaid month, and include unpaid subcontract retainage in Claim amount if the subcontract provides for it. Invoices behind the form should split progress vs retainage.

The day Click’s work on that job is complete — or the day you treat the subcontract as terminated or abandoned — start the 30-day § 53.057 clock. Do not wait for the GC to declare completion.

Use the statutory retainage form with the WARNING block. Send owner and GC by traceable service.

Watch for an owner affidavit of completion (§ 53.106) or a termination notice (§ 53.107). Calendar 30 days from original-contract completion; after that the owner may legally release the 10% if no proper claim is on file.

File the affidavit on the earlier applicable date: § 53.052(d) (15th day of the third month after the month the original contract ended) and the older § 53.103 thirty-day language. Treat the first date as the deadline.

Serve the filed affidavit under § 53.055. For a pure § 53.057 trap, that service is what lets the owner withhold.

What Click should not expect
You do not trap 10% of the GC’s entire prime contract for Click’s plumbing invoice. You trap the claim in the notice, and you share the owner’s 10% reserved fund with every other unpaid claimant on that same original contract.

Owner A’s reserved funds cannot pay Click on Owner B’s house. A multi-property GC failure is a stack of single-property files.

If the owner already paid the GC in full and already released reserved funds after the 30-day hold, there is nothing left to trap. What remains is a § 53.105 failure-to-reserve argument plus the lien, if timely.

A payment bond on that project changes the path. Check each job for a bond before you tell an owner to hold 10%.

Cover letter on a retainage notice follows the same rules: own page, no joint check, no GC smear, copy the GC. Ask the owner to hold the reserved 10% and Click’s subcontract retainage and not release either to the GC. If the packet is § 53.057-only, say the § 53.081(c) withhold starts when they receive a copy of the affidavit.
Section 5 demand letters — leave them off this envelope
Chapter 28’s 1.5% and CPRC Chapter 38 presentment belong on the demand to the party in privity — the GC on these jobs, or the homeowner only when Click is the original contractor. Penal Code § 31.04 stays off by default. Putting any of that on the owner’s § 53.056 packet makes the notice look like a collection threat and does not make the owner more likely to trap funds. Use a separate GC demand for interest and fees.
