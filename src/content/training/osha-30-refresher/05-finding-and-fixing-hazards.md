## Why it matters
This is the field toolkit. Spot the hazard, weigh the risk, pick the control, and learn from every incident and near miss. The JHA, the job hazard analysis, is where it comes together.

## Must know
- Identify hazards when the work or site changes, at set intervals, and after a near miss or incident. Also on new information or a worker's concern.
- The tools: root cause analysis, worker input, inspections, SDSs, the chemical safety sheets, and observation.
- A hazard is the source of harm. Risk is how likely the harm is, weighed against how severe.
- A SIF, a serious injury or fatality, is a death, a life-threatening injury or a life-altering one. Minor cuts, uninjured slips, delays and outages are not SIFs.
- High-risk work: heights, electricity, struck-by, trenching, confined spaces and cranes.
- Mitigation gives each control an owner with authority and fixes the highest risk first. Workers fix housekeeping within their own span of control.
- Then validate. The controls exist, the training is current and the PPE is actually worn.
- A JHA lists each step, its hazards and its controls, with the workers. A serious hazard found during the JHA means stop work and install temporary controls.
- {{figure:controls-ladder|The controls in a JHA come from this ladder. Reach for the top rungs first.}}
- Revise the JHA on any change, like weather, a new chemical or a new site. Never reuse another site's JHA.
- An incident is a work-related injury, illness, death or property damage. A near miss differs only by luck and is reported too.
- Investigations are fact-finding, not fault-finding. Preserve the scene, collect the facts, find the root cause, correct it and verify.
- In the case, an untrained worker fell from an **8-ft** Type 1A stepladder rated **300 lb**. The fix was removing the climb and training, not PPE or blame.

## Traps
- Hazard is the source of harm. Risk is likelihood against severity.
- Reprimanding, threatening or blaming is always the wrong answer.
- A forklift working in rain needs the JHA revised for weather, not a slower speed or retraining.

## Numbers
- **8 ft** the stepladder in the case
- **300 lb** its Type 1A rating
- **3** kinds of SIF, death, life-threatening, life-altering
- **6** kinds of high-risk work

## Check yourself
1. Rain made the truck bed and pavement slippery, a hazard the JHA did not cover. How could they have improved safety that morning?
- [ ] Adjust the forklift's speed
- [ ] Give extra training
- [x] Revise the JHA for dangerous weather
- [ ] Get a new forklift
> right: A JHA is revised whenever a new hazard shows up, and weather counts.
> wrong: Speed, training and a new machine miss the point. The plan has to change when the hazard changes.

2. In the JHA for an electrician wiring a store, which hazard is the most dangerous?
- [ ] Sparks and fire
- [x] Electrical shock from live wires
- [ ] Falls
- [ ] Repetitive strain
> right: Live wires can kill. Risk weighs how severe the harm is, not just how likely.
> wrong: Weigh severity. Touching a live conductor means shock, burns or death, worse than the other three.

3. What should employers do if they find caught-in or between hazards?
- [ ] Ignore them
- [ ] Tell workers to be careful
- [ ] Report them to OSHA only
- [x] Eliminate or address the hazards
> right: Mitigation means removing or reducing the hazard, with an owner and a deadline.
> wrong: Warnings and reports do not remove the hazard. The employer fixes it or controls it.

4. Which is an example of an engineering control for lifting heavy objects?
- [ ] A written procedure
- [x] A mechanical lift or hoist
- [ ] Training
- [ ] Back belts
> right: The machine takes the load off the body without relying on behavior.
> wrong: Procedures and training are administrative, a back belt is PPE. Engineering changes the task itself.
