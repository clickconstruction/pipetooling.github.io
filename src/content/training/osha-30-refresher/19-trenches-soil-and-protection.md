## Why it matters
Trench collapses kill about **40** workers a year. Most happen in trenches only **5 to 15 feet** deep. The right protection depends on reading the soil right.

## Must know
- A trench is deeper than it is wide, and no more than **15 feet** wide at the bottom.
- The competent person classifies the soil and inspects the trench **daily**, before anyone enters.
- They inspect again after rain, vibration or any other event. They can stop the work.
- Under **5 feet**, a protective system is needed only if the competent person sees cave-in potential.
- From **5 to 20 feet**, the system follows OSHA's tables, a maker's data or an engineer's design.
- Deeper than **20 feet**, only a registered professional engineer may design the system.
- Type A is cohesive clay at **1.5 tsf** or more. Type B sits between **0.5 and 1.5 tsf**. Type C is **0.5 tsf** or less, sand, gravel or wet soil.
- Fissured, vibrated, disturbed or layered soil can never be Type A. Use the least stable class you find.
- Slopes: Type A at **¾ to 1**, Type B at **1 to 1**, Type C at **1½ to 1**. Benching is allowed in A and B, never in C.
- The system reaches **18 inches** above the top of the trench. Spoil and tools stay at least **2 feet** back from the edge.
- A trench box protects the people inside it. It does not stop the soil from moving.

## Traps
- Clumpy clay beside a busy road is Type B. The vibration rules out Type A.
- Keep the depths apart. Egress and air testing at 4 feet, a system at 5, edge fall protection at 6, an engineer past 20.

## Numbers
- **5 ft** a protective system
- **20 ft** an engineer
- **18 in** above the top
- **2 ft** spoil back from the edge
- **15 ft** the widest a trench can be
- **1.5 tsf** Type A
- **0.5 tsf** Type C
- **daily** the inspection

- [ ] Type B

> right: Type C will not hold a step. Slope it or box it.
> wrong: Benching works in A and B. Type C is the one that cannot hold a step.
