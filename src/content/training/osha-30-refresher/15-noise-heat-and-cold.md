## Why it matters
Noise, vibration, heat and cold are hazards you usually cannot see. Construction workers are **13 times** more likely to die of heat illness than workers in other industries.

## Must know
- Sound above **85 dBA** destroys the hair cells in the cochlea, the inner ear. The loss is temporary at first and permanent with continued exposure.
- A nail gun is **95**, a jackhammer **105**, a chainsaw **110 dBA**. If you must raise your voice to be heard at **2 to 3 ft**, it is probably over 85.
- An 8-hour TWA, the time-weighted average, of 85 dBA starts a hearing conservation program. That means noise monitoring, free hearing protection, training, records, and baseline and annual hearing tests.
- **90 dBA** as an 8-hour TWA is the PEL, the permissible exposure limit. Above it you need hearing protection plus engineering or administrative controls.
- Unprotected time: **8 h** at 90, **4 h** at 95, **2 h** at 100 and **1 h** at 105 dBA. At 110 it is **30 min**, and at 115 only **15 min**.
- Hand-arm vibration from handheld tools is the bigger injury source. Up to **50%** of exposed workers develop hand-arm vibration syndrome: tingling, numbness, white fingertips. Controls: low-vibration tools, anti-vibration gloves, breaks, rotation and maintenance.
- Heat is a hazard in any season, indoors or out. Acclimatization takes **1 to 2 weeks**, and skipping it is a major factor in heat deaths. Hydrate all day, take breaks at least every **2 hours**, and seek shade.
- Heat illness runs rash, cramps, exhaustion, then heat stroke at around **104°F** with confusion and hot dry skin. Call **911**, move the person to shade and cool them.
- Cold risk combines temperature, wind and wetness, and wet feet lose heat **25 times** faster. Hypothermia is a body temperature below **95°F**: shivering, confusion, slurred speech. Treat it with a warm area, dry clothing, medical care and warm sweet drinks, never alcohol.
- Site radiation is non-ionizing: power lines, radio frequency, infrared, ultraviolet from the sun and welding arcs, and lasers. Keep exposure as low as reasonably achievable with time, distance and shielding.
- Lasers are for qualified operators only, with laser eyewear when reflections can exceed **5 mW**. Cap a laser when idle and never aim it at people or reflective surfaces.

## Traps
- 85 dBA starts the hearing conservation program. 90 dBA is the PEL that requires protection plus controls.
- Sunscreen prevents sunburn, not heat stroke.
- The worst cold is not always the lowest temperature. 35°F with 20 mph gusts in a sweat-soaked shirt beats a calm 25°F with hat and gloves.
- Ordinary safety glasses do not stop lasers.

## Numbers
- **85 dBA** hearing conservation program, and the damage line
- **90 dBA** the noise PEL
- **4 h** unprotected at 95 dBA
- **50%** of exposed workers get hand-arm vibration syndrome
- **1 to 2 weeks** to acclimatize to heat
- **104°F** heat stroke
- **2 hours** the longest between breaks in the heat
- **95°F** body temperature, hypothermia below it
- **5 mW** laser eyewear above it

## Check yourself
1. What is an effective practice against loud worksite noise?
- [ ] Trade earplugs with a coworker
- [ ] Ignore short bursts of noise
- [x] Wear employer-provided hearing protection
- [ ] Shout over the noise
> right: If the noise cannot be removed, everyone in the loud area wears protection.
> wrong: Short bursts still damage the ear. The fix is the hearing protection your employer gives you.

2. How can an employer protect workers on extremely hot days?
- [x] Increase outdoor time gradually to acclimatize
- [ ] Have them wear extra layers
- [ ] Have them work full shifts right away
- [ ] Have them skip breaks
> right: Acclimatization takes 1 to 2 weeks. Skipping it is a major factor in heat deaths.
> wrong: New workers need 1 to 2 weeks to get used to heat. Layers and skipped breaks make it worse.

3. Which circumstance poses the greatest cold-stress risk?
- [ ] A calm sunny 25°F day with hat and gloves
- [ ] A cloudy 32°F day, 5 mph wind, a winter jacket
- [ ] A partly sunny 30°F day, 20 mph wind, a windbreaker
- [x] A 35°F day, 20 mph gusts, a sweat-soaked shirt
> right: Wind and wet beat a low number. The gusts and the soaked shirt pull heat out fastest.
> wrong: Cold risk is temperature, wind and wetness together. The wet shirt in the wind loses the most heat.

4. Which radiation type is commonly found on construction sites?
- [ ] Gamma
- [x] Non-ionizing
- [ ] X-rays
- [ ] Alpha
> right: Sun, welding arcs, power lines and lasers are all non-ionizing.
> wrong: Gamma, X-rays and alpha are ionizing. Site radiation is the other kind: sun, arcs, lines and lasers.
