## Why it matters
Most electrical deaths fall on workers in non-electrical trades. **53%** of electrical fatalities are in construction, and **69%** involve workers outside the electrical trades. NFPA 70E is the "how" behind OSHA's electrical rules.

## Must know
- NFPA 70E is the Standard for Electrical Safety in the Workplace. OSHA is the law. 70E is guidance that OSHA often cites.
- It has three chapters and **19** non-mandatory annexes. It is revised **every 3 years**, and the current edition is **2024**.
- The three hazards are shock, arc flash and arc blast. Arc flash is heat and light. Arc blast is a pressure wave and flying debris.
- De-energizing is 70E's first priority, using LOTO, lockout tagout. Energized work is justified only to test voltage, to troubleshoot, or when shutting down is impossible or more dangerous. Never to save time.
- The ESP, the electrical safety program, is written, site-specific and created by the employer. It covers planning, procedures, LOTO, PPE, near-miss reporting and training. It is audited **every 3 years**.
- A qualified person has demonstrated knowledge of the specific equipment plus hazard training. Only they test, troubleshoot, measure voltage or work energized.
- Qualification is per task and per equipment. A license or experience does not make someone qualified. Supervision checks it, with an **annual** inspection and retraining **at least every 3 years**.
- An unqualified worker learns to recognize hazards, follow safe practices, understand approach boundaries and know when they are not qualified.
- The arc flash boundary is where an arc could burn you. The limited approach boundary is for shock. An unqualified person crosses either only while continuously escorted by a qualified person. The restricted boundary is qualified workers only, in shock PPE with insulated tools.
- Arc flash PPE categories: 1 is **4 cal/cm²**, 2 is **8**, 3 is **25**, 4 is **40**. Wear it over natural fibers, because synthetics melt. Read the equipment's arc flash label and never reuse yesterday's choice.

- Shock protection is rubber insulating gloves under leather protectors, dielectric boots, mats, blankets and tools rated **1,000 V**. Check the test date on the gloves. Inspect all of it before each day's use and after any incident.
- Near exposed parts, enforce the boundaries, follow LOTO and read the labels. Poor lighting is a major cause of electrical accidents.

## Traps
- A licensed electrician, or a subcontractor's experienced crew, is not automatically qualified.
- A rubber-coated tool prevents electricity from traveling through the tool. "Reduces the flow" is marked wrong.
- NFPA 70E is not a law.
- Three things recur every 3 years: 70E revisions, ESP audits and qualified-person retraining.

## Check yourself
1. Which of the following is true about an ESP, the electrical safety program?
- [ ] A plan that NFPA 70E provides
- [x] A written, site-specific plan by the employer
- [ ] A plan workers follow in the parts they pick
- [ ] Practices written after an accident
> right: 70E guides the program, but the employer writes it for its own site.
> wrong: 70E does not hand you the program. Your employer writes one for this site, and all of it applies.

2. Which of the following does NFPA 70E require workers to do?
- [ ] Provide the ESP training
- [ ] Report every near miss to OSHA
- [x] Follow the employer's safety procedures
- [ ] Assess the hazards on the jobsite
> right: Training, hazard assessment and the program itself are the employer's duties.
> wrong: Training people, assessing the site and reporting to OSHA are the employer's jobs. Yours is to follow the procedures.

3. Which of these is not a reason to keep an electrical system energized?
- [x] To save time
- [ ] To troubleshoot the equipment
- [ ] To test the voltage
- [ ] To keep critical systems running
> right: Being behind schedule never justifies energized work. De-energize and lock it out.
> wrong: Testing, troubleshooting and life-safety systems can justify it. The clock never does.

4. Which of these is not expected of an unqualified person?
- [ ] Recognize electrical hazards
- [ ] Know when they are not qualified
- [ ] Understand safe approach distances
- [x] De-energize systems
> right: Shutting a system down and verifying it is dead is a qualified person's work.
> wrong: An unqualified worker recognizes hazards, keeps the distances and knows their limits. Shutting the system down is a qualified person's job.
