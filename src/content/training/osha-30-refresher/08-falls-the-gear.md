## Why it matters
Almost **50%** of fatal falls happen with no protection in use. Roofs cause **33%** and ladders **23.5%**, over half together. About **20%** of fatal falls are from **10 ft** or less.

## Must know
- A leading edge is an edge that moves as the work goes on. Only a restraint or an arrest system can protect it.
- Passive systems need no action from you: guardrails, nets and covers. Active systems are PPE you wear, and they need training.
- Guardrail top rail at **42 in ± 3 in**. Midrail at **21 in**. Posts take **200 lb**. Add a toeboard wherever objects could fall.
- {{figure:guardrail|The top rail at the hip, the midrail at the knee, the toeboard at the ankle.}}
- A parapet is the short wall at a roof edge. A **24-in** parapet can serve as midrail and toeboard under an added top rail.
- The competent person inspects guardrails before each use. You inspect your PFAS before every use and tag damaged gear Do Not Use.
- Safety nets go under work over **25 ft**. Mesh no larger than **6 by 6 in**. Inspected **weekly**. Never more than **30 ft** below the work.
- The ABCs of a PFAS: Anchorage, Body harness, Connecting device. The anchorage holds **5,000 lb** per worker, about a pickup truck. Never conduit, pipes or scaffold cross braces.
- The harness fits you and is never shared. The back D-ring sits between the shoulder blades. Arresting force at most **1,800 lb**.
- Snap hooks are self-closing and self-locking. A shock-absorbing lanyard allows about **6 ft** of free fall. A self-retracting lanyard locks within about **2 ft**.
- Free fall is the lanyard length plus or minus the distance from D-ring to anchor. It is never over **6 ft**. A 6-ft lanyard anchored at your feet means **11 ft**.
- Clearance adds four things to the free fall. **3.5 ft** to slow, **1 ft** of D-ring shift, **5 ft** of D-ring height and **2 ft** of safety. Rule of thumb: **18 ft** below you.
- {{figure:clearance-stack|Five distances stacked under the anchor. With less than 18 ft of clear space, the harness cannot save you.}}
- The fall protection plan drives the training. An in-house competent person gives it and records it. It repeats when hazards change or after a fall.

## Traps
- Free fall is the thing you control most. An anchor below your D-ring adds to it. Anchor overhead or use a self-retracting lanyard.
- Nets do not prevent a fall. A stationary system cannot follow a leading edge.
- Three wrong setups. A snap hook choked back on its own strap. A cord wrapped around the lanyard. A plain lanyard, with no shock absorber, used for arrest.
- Fraying stitching means the harness comes out of service now. Not after the task, not at the end of the day.

## Numbers
- **42 in** top rail
- **21 in** midrail
- **200 lb** a guardrail post holds
- **25 ft** nets go under work this high
- **5,000 lb** the anchorage, per worker
- **1,800 lb** the most a harness may put on you
- **6 ft** the most free fall
- **2 ft** a self-retracting lanyard locks
- **18 ft** clearance rule of thumb
- **weekly** net inspection

## Check yourself
1. An anchorage holds 5,000 lb per worker. What weighs about that much?
- [ ] A refrigerator
- [ ] A grand piano
- [x] A pickup truck
- [ ] A buffalo
> right: Ask of every tie-off point, would this hold a pickup truck?
> wrong: A fridge is about 400 lb and a buffalo about 2,200 lb. The anchor must hold something far heavier.

2. You are rushed and find fraying stitching on your harness. What do you do?
- [ ] Finish your tasks first
- [x] Take the harness out of service
- [ ] Report it at the end of the day
- [ ] Fix it yourself
> right: Even minor damage can fail in a fall. Tag it Do Not Use and tell your manager.
> wrong: Damaged gear leaves service the moment you find it. Then your manager hears about it.

3. A crew installs roof panels along a leading edge. Which fall protection can protect them?
- [ ] Guardrails
- [ ] Hole covers
- [x] Fall restraint
- [ ] Barricades
> right: Only restraint or arrest moves with the edge as the work moves.
> wrong: A fixed barrier cannot follow an edge that moves as the panels go on. The protection has to be worn.

4. What primarily guides the content of a worksite's fall protection training?
- [ ] Topics the competent person likes teaching
- [ ] Borrowed lesson plans
- [ ] What workers find interesting
- [x] The fall protection plan
> right: The plan keeps the training site-specific.
> wrong: Training comes from the written plan for this site, not from anyone's preferences.
