## Why it matters
Every module of the course comes back to the same few ideas. They cover the strongest controls, who is in charge, what your employer owes you and when to stop work. Know these and the rest of the course makes sense.

## Must know
- Controls run from most to least effective. Elimination, substitution, engineering, administrative, then PPE, the gear you wear.
- {{figure:controls-ladder|Start at the top. Each step down leans more on people doing the right thing.}}
- Engineering controls work without you. Guards, ventilation and dust collection are engineering, while training and permits are administrative.
- PPE is the last line of defense. It reduces the effect of a hazard and never removes it. A respirator, the mask you breathe through, is PPE too.
- The competent person is named by the employer. They spot existing and predictable hazards and can stop work.
- The qualified person has proven skill on that task and that equipment. A license or years alone do not make one.
- A registered professional engineer designs trench protection, the protective system against falling walls, deeper than **20 ft**. A fire watch stays **30 minutes** after hot work ends.
- A signal person is certified and is needed when the crane operator cannot see the load. A spotter prevents collisions and needs no certification.
- Your employer owes a workplace free of recognized hazards, training you understand, and PPE at no cost. Your duties are to follow the rules, inspect your gear, and report hazards instead of fixing them yourself. NFPA 70E, the electrical safety standard, splits the duties the same way.
- A death is reported to OSHA within **8 hours**. A hospital stay, an amputation or a lost eye within **24 hours**.
- Employers with **more than 10** employees keep the OSHA 300 log. The 300A summary is posted **Feb 1 to Apr 30** and kept **5 years**.
- You may speak up, ask for an inspection, report an injury and see your records. A retaliation complaint goes to OSHA within **30 days**.
- Any employee at any level can stop unsafe work. An unsafe scaffold, the platform built up to work from, is one case. Raise it with your supervisor first, then the next level, then OSHA.

## Traps
- Safety training is an administrative control. A barrier is an engineering control.
- A license or years on the job do not make a qualified person. Qualification is per task and per machine.
- Refusal is protected only with a real danger of death or serious injury and no time to fix it. Report and wait, never walk off.
- A hazard goes to your supervisor first. A coworker's unsafe act is raised with that coworker first, not their manager or OSHA.

## Numbers
- **20 ft** an engineer designs the trench protection
- **30 min** the fire watch stays after hot work
- **8 hours** to report a death to OSHA
- **24 hours** to report a hospital stay, an amputation or a lost eye
- **more than 10** employees keep an OSHA 300 log
- **Feb 1 to Apr 30** the 300A summary is posted
- **5 years** the records are kept
- **30 days** to file a retaliation complaint

## Check yourself
1. Which hazard control method involves changing the way people work?
- [ ] Engineering controls
- [x] Administrative controls
- [ ] PPE
- [ ] Elimination
> right: Administrative controls change what people do. Training, permits and work rules are the examples.
> wrong: Engineering changes the machine, PPE covers the body and elimination removes the hazard. Changing how people work is administrative.

2. What is the employer's responsibility if a trench is deeper than 20 feet?
- [ ] Provide extra tools
- [ ] Reduce the number of workers
- [x] Have a registered professional engineer design the protective system
- [ ] Offer more breaks
> right: Past 20 ft the protection is designed by a registered professional engineer, never improvised.
> wrong: Tools, fewer workers and breaks do not hold up a wall. Past 20 ft an engineer designs the protection.

3. Which of the following does NFPA 70E, the electrical safety standard, require workers to do?
- [x] Follow the employer's electrical safety procedures
- [ ] Provide the safety program training
- [ ] Report near misses to OSHA
- [ ] Assess the jobsite hazards
> right: Training and hazard assessment are the employer's duties. Yours is to follow the procedures.
> wrong: The employer trains and assesses. The worker follows the program and reports near misses to the manager, not OSHA.

4. You are told to work on a scaffold you believe is unsafe. What do you do?
- [ ] Stay quiet
- [x] Talk to your manager about your concerns
- [ ] Go straight to OSHA
- [ ] Assume the builders know what they are doing
> right: Raise it with your manager first. OSHA comes after the employer fails to act.
> wrong: Staying quiet or trusting the builders fixes nothing. The employer gets the first chance, OSHA the next.
