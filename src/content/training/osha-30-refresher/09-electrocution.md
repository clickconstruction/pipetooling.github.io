## Why it matters
Electricity is a leading cause of construction deaths. Almost every electrical injury on a site could be seen coming.

## Must know
- You get shocked when you become a path. Touch two parts of a circuit, or touch a live conductor while grounded.
- Electrocution is a shock that kills. BE SAFE: Burns, Electrocution, Shock, Arc flash, Fires, Explosions. Burns are the most common injury.
- Severity depends on the current, its path and how long it lasts. Across the chest is worst. See a doctor after any shock.
- **1 mA** is a tingle. **5 mA** is a slight shock. About **30 mA** locks your muscles so you cannot let go. **50 to 150 mA** stops breathing. **200 mA** can stop the heart.
- A breaker trips at **15,000 mA**. It protects the wiring, not you.
- The ground prong carries fault current to earth. A double-insulated tool shows a square within a square.
- A GFCI, the ground fault circuit interrupter, trips at **5 mA** in **1/40 s**. It protects only what is plugged in downstream of it.
- GFCIs are required on every temporary **120 V, 15 A and 20 A** receptacle. Test them **monthly**.
- Inspect tools before each use. Never carry or unplug a tool by the cord. No wet areas, homemade cords, two-prong adapters or cut-off ground prongs.
- Arc flash is current jumping a gap through the air. Live work only when de-energizing is infeasible or more dangerous, with a permit, arc-rated PPE and insulated tools.
- Overhead lines are always live. Stay **10 ft** away up to **50 kV** and **15 ft** up to 200 kV. Then **20 ft** to 350 kV and **25 ft** to 500 kV. Past that, **35 ft** to 750 kV and **45 ft** to 1,000 kV.
- {{figure:power-line-clearances|The higher the voltage, the further you stay. Ten feet is the least, and that is for the smallest line.}}
- Underground flags: **yellow** gas or oil, **blue** water, **red** electric. The employer calls **811**. You verify the marks and use hand tools within **3 ft** of a flag.

## Traps
- A disconnected panel is not safe. Stored energy or a coworker re-energizing it can still shock you. Lock out, tag out, then try it.
- A metal tool in your hand or a breaker nearby is not the hazard. Damaged cords, wet conditions, power lines and lightning are.
- A GFCI near the work will not trip for damage on the cord that feeds it.
- A tool that runs warm may be leaking current. Tag it out.

## Numbers
- **5 mA** a GFCI trips
- **30 mA** you cannot let go
- **200 mA** can stop the heart
- **15,000 mA** a breaker trips
- **10 ft** from lines up to 50 kV
- **3 ft** hand tools only near a flag
- **811** before any digging
- **monthly** test the GFCI

## Check yourself
1. Why can a squirrel run along a power line unharmed?
- [ ] It is small
- [x] It is not grounded
- [ ] Its fur insulates it
- [ ] The line carries little energy
> right: No path to ground, so no current flows through it. Touch the pole too and it becomes the circuit.
> wrong: Size, fur and line energy do not matter. What matters is whether current has a path through the body.

2. A drill has a frayed cord with exposed wires. What do you do?
- [ ] Tape the cord
- [ ] Use it carefully
- [x] Do not use it
- [ ] Plug it into a GFCI
> right: It goes back to work only after a fix or a replacement.
> wrong: Tape, care and a GFCI do not mend an exposed wire. The tool leaves service until it is fixed or replaced.

3. Who makes sure workers get arc flash training?
- [ ] Workers, paying for it themselves
- [ ] OSHA
- [x] The employer
- [ ] The manufacturer
> right: Workers must take part and follow the procedures. The duty to train is the employer's.
> wrong: OSHA sets the rule and makers build the gear. Training the crew is the company's job.

4. A worker is about to break a slab near underground utilities. Which part is the worker's job?
- [ ] Call 811 to mark the lines
- [ ] Get maps of the underground services
- [x] Verify where the lines are
- [ ] Provide detection equipment
> right: The employer calls 811 and gets the maps. You check the marks and know where they are.
> wrong: Calling 811, the maps and the detectors are the employer's. Your part is checking the marks before you dig.
