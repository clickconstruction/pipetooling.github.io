## Why it matters
Carbon monoxide is the one thing in this course that kills with no warning. You cannot see it, smell it or taste it. It comes from a flame short of air or a vent that spills. The first sign is often a headache that everyone in the house shares.

## Must know
- Carbon monoxide, CO for short, is a poison gas. It has no colour, no smell and no taste.
- A flame with enough air makes very little CO. A flame short of air makes a lot.
- CO reaches people when the flame's gas spills into the room. A blocked vent or a cracked heat exchanger does it.
- {{figure:co-path|A starved flame makes carbon monoxide. A blocked vent or a cracked heat exchanger lets it into the room.}}
- The first signs are headache, tiredness, dizziness, drowsiness and feeling sick. More of it brings confusion and collapse.
- CO is measured in parts per million, ppm. OSHA's limit for a work day is **50 ppm** averaged over 8 hours.
- At **1,200 ppm** the air is an immediate danger to life.
- The code wants a CO alarm in a house with a fuel-burning appliance or an attached garage.
- The alarm goes outside each sleeping area, near the bedrooms.
- A home alarm is slow on purpose. At **70 ppm** it may take one to four hours to sound.
- {{figure:co-scale|OSHA's limit is 50 ppm over a work day. A home alarm can take hours at 70. At 1,200 the air can kill.}}
- When an alarm sounds, everyone goes outside to fresh air. Then call 911 from outside.

## Traps
- No smell does not mean no gas. The smell added to natural gas is not in carbon monoxide.
- A quiet alarm is not a clean reading. A home alarm ignores low levels that a technician's meter will show.
- Opening a window is not the fix. The people leave first, and the cause is found before anyone goes back.

## Numbers
- **50 ppm** is OSHA's limit, averaged over 8 hours
- **1,200 ppm** is an immediate danger to life
- **70 ppm** can take a home alarm one to four hours to sound
