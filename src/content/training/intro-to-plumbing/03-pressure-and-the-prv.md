## Why it matters
Pressure is the push behind every faucet. Too little and the shower dribbles. Too much and fittings, hoses and the water heater fail. The PRV, the pressure reducing valve, holds the push where the house wants it.

## Must know
- Pressure is measured in psi, pounds per square inch. A gauge screwed onto a hose bib reads it.
- {{figure:pressure-and-prv|The gauge reads the push. The PRV lets high street pressure through as a lower house pressure.}}
- **40 to 60 psi** feels good at the shower and is kind to the pipes.
- Under **40 psi** the house feels weak. Two fixtures running at once make it worse.
- **80 psi** is the most the code allows in a house. Above it, a PRV is required.
- A PRV sits on the service line after the main shutoff. A spring on top sets the house side.
- The PRV lets the street's high pressure in and sends a steady lower pressure on.
- Every **2.31 ft** of height costs **1 psi**. A second floor reads about 4 psi lower than the first.
- A PRV makes the house a closed system. An expansion tank then gives heated water room to grow.

## Traps
- High pressure feels like a strong shower. The damage shows up as drips, bangs and a leaking water heater.
- A PRV that fails can fail high or low. A gauge tells you which, not the feel at the tap.
- The PRV does not stop the water. The main shutoff does.

## Numbers
- **40 to 60 psi** comfortable
- **80 psi** the most the code allows
- **a PRV** above 80
- **2.31 ft** of height per psi

## Check yourself
1. What does a PRV do?
- [ ] Stops the water
- [x] Lowers high street pressure to a steady house pressure
- [ ] Heats the water
- [ ] Counts the gallons
> right: It takes the street's push and sends on a lower, steadier one.
> wrong: A PRV neither stops nor measures. It reduces pressure to the house.

2. Above what pressure does the code require a PRV?
- [ ] 40 psi
- [ ] 60 psi
- [x] 80 psi
- [ ] 100 psi
> right: 80 psi is the ceiling in a house. Above it the PRV goes in.
> wrong: The code's ceiling is 80 psi. Street pressure above it needs a PRV.

3. How do you find out a house's pressure?
- [ ] Feel the shower
- [ ] Listen to the pipes
- [x] Screw a gauge onto a hose bib
- [ ] Ask the meter
> right: The gauge reads the push. The shower only hints.
> wrong: Feel and sound hint. A gauge on a hose bib reads the number.

4. Why does a second floor shower read lower than the first?
- [x] Height costs pressure, about 1 psi every 2.31 ft
- [ ] The pipe is smaller upstairs
- [ ] Hot water weighs less
- [ ] The PRV is upstairs
> right: Water has to be lifted. Every 2.31 ft up costs 1 psi.
> wrong: It is the height. Lifting water up a floor costs about 4 psi.
